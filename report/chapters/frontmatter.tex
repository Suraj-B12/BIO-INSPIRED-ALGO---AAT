\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
Tomato plants suffer from many leaf diseases, and spotting them early helps farmers protect their crop. This project builds a computer model that looks at a photo of a tomato leaf and names one of ten classes (nine diseases or healthy). We use EfficientNet-B0, a compact image-recognition network already trained on millions of everyday photos (ImageNet), and adapt it to tomato leaves. Reusing a trained network in this way is called transfer learning.

How well such a model learns depends on its \emph{hyperparameters}: the training settings we choose before training, such as the learning rate and how much of the network is retrained. Instead of guessing them, we search for them with \emph{Differential Evolution (DE)}, a search method inspired by natural evolution that improves a group of candidate settings over generations. We wrote DE ourselves in NumPy and first checked it on five standard test functions against four other search methods.

The cleaned PlantVillage tomato dataset has \TotalN\ images, split into \TrainN\ for training, \ValN\ for validation (used to choose settings), and \TestN\ for the final test. We compare three models: a fixed default recipe (the baseline), the DE-tuned recipe, and a recipe found by random search with the same number of trials.

DE raises average test accuracy from \BaseAcc\% to \DEAcc\% and macro-F1 (the average score across all disease classes) from \BaseFone\% to \DEFone\%. This removes \ErrorReduction\% of the baseline's mistakes, and every class improves. A statistical test (McNemar, $p=\McNemarP$) confirms that the improvement is not due to chance. Random search also reaches \RSAcc\%, which confirms that the gain comes from tuning the training recipe. Heat-map explanations (Grad-CAM) and an offline web demo show what the models look at and how the search evolves.
