## Spoken script and visual cues

### 1. Opening — Aryanraj

“We are Team 10. We study whether Differential Evolution can improve the training recipe of an EfficientNet-B0 tomato classifier. We built the optimizer, the evaluation pipeline, and an offline interface for inspecting predictions and search behavior. Our guide is Manoj Kumar S, Assistant Professor.”

### 2. Define the optimization problem — Aryanraj

“An optimization problem has decisions, an objective, and constraints. Our decisions include learning rate, regularization, head width, and fine-tuning depth. The objective is validation macro-F1. A candidate costs an actual training run, and this mixed recipe cannot be optimized directly by differentiating the validation score.”

### 3. Explain population search — Aryanraj

“A population maintains several possible solutions. Variation explores new recipes, while selection retains useful ones. Exploration seeks new regions and exploitation refines promising ones. DE uses numerical differences between candidates to propose steps. Its operators are inspectable, but it does not guarantee a global optimum.”

### 4. Locate DE within the field — Aryanraj

“GA, DE, and evolution strategies are evolutionary methods. PSO, ACO, and ABC are swarm methods. Simulated annealing uses a physics analogy; BBO uses biogeography. Categories overlap. We judge mechanisms and measured behavior, not the metaphor. In particular, binomial crossover makes the full DE algorithm coordinate-dependent.”

Handoff: “Richik will explain the data and the baseline fixed before search.”

### 5. State the application and scope — Richik

“The fixed application is the color tomato subset of PlantVillage. The cleaned count on the slide comes from the preparation output. Exact duplicates are removed and perceptually similar groups stay in one split. These controlled leaf images support reproducible evaluation, but they do not establish performance on arbitrary field photographs.”

### 6. Trace the evidence flow — Richik

“Follow the pipeline from verified images to the fixed baseline, DE and random search, validation reranking, repeated final training, and the locked test. Both searches use equal candidate budgets and the same proxy conditions. Final recipes are frozen before test evaluation. The demo loads the exact seed-zero checkpoints.”

Point to the baseline path and the separate reranking paths. Do not imply that the baseline is selected by reranking.

### 7. Explain the registered baseline — Richik

“The baseline freezes EfficientNet-B0 and trains a 256-unit head with dropout 0.2, Adam at 0.001, and no weight decay or label smoothing. It uses the common crop-and-flip pipeline without extra RandAugment. Candidate recipes use AdamW. Therefore, the baseline comparison concerns the whole recipe; DE versus random search more directly compares proposal strategies.”

Handoff: “Suraj will explain the operators and the seven decisions they evolve.”

### 8. Walk through the DE operators — Suraj

“For each target, choose three distinct donors, all excluding that target. Mutation adds F times a donor difference to the third donor. Repair an out-of-range coordinate between its parent and violated bound. Binomial crossover takes mutant coordinates with probability CR and forces at least one mutant coordinate. Selection accepts an equal or better validation score.”

Point to the operator example. “We test these invariants directly. Each completed evaluation persists the population and random-generator state so an interrupted run can resume.”

### 9. Decode the chromosome — Suraj

“All seven genes lie between zero and one. Learning rate and weight decay decode logarithmically; head width, unfreezing depth, and augmentation are discrete. A zero-width head is a direct linear classifier. Augmentation zero disables extra RandAugment, retaining the common crop-and-flip transforms. Population, F, CR, and the evaluation budget are fixed before search.”

Point to the two logarithmic rows and the printed budget; avoid reading every bound aloud.

### 10. Distinguish search fitness from final performance — Suraj

“DE finishes with higher proxy validation fitness, but random search has the higher reranked validation score. Both used equal candidate budgets, a shared training seed, and the same subset. After final training, they tie in mean test accuracy. The proxy ranking therefore does not establish a final DE advantage.”

### 11. Read the measured comparison — Suraj

“Across all three registered training seeds, baseline accuracy is 97.43%; DE and random search both reach 99.85%. Their macro-F1 values both round to 99.80%, versus baseline 96.60%. Both searched recipes improve clean classification, but this experiment does not establish DE superiority over random search. Robustness and calibration tell a less favorable story.”

Handoff: “Sushant will show the remaining errors and deployment trade-offs.”

### 12. Inspect confusion patterns — Sushant

“Rows are true classes and cells are fractions. The baseline’s largest confusion is Target Spot predicted as spider mites. DE removes that pattern, but still confuses Early and Late blight and sometimes calls Early blight Target Spot. Disease names alone do not establish why these errors occur.”

### 13. Keep every class visible — Sushant

“Mean F1 improves over the baseline in every class. Early blight and Target Spot have the largest gains; Early blight remains the weakest DE class. These averages include all three seeds. The random-search recipe reaches nearly identical overall performance.”

### 14. Inspect Grad-CAM explanations — Sushant

“Both displayed leaves are classified correctly. On the Early blight leaf, DE’s map covers more central leaf area, while the baseline emphasizes a lower boundary and background. That difference is observable, but it does not prove lesion localization. The maps are normalized within each image.”

### 15. Read the corruption stress test — Sushant

“DE’s mean corruption accuracy is 69.19%, below baseline 85.77%. It handles blur and JPEG better, but noise and stronger lighting changes expose major weaknesses. These are identical seed-zero stress tests. Clean accuracy gains do not imply greater field robustness.”

### 16. Live demonstration — Sushant

**First 25 seconds:** Open the local demo, press **P** if needed, select an included leaf, and analyze it. Point to both predictions, top-three probabilities, and the Grad-CAM maps that appear automatically. Probabilities apply to the known tomato classes.

**Next 15 seconds:** Apply mild blur and analyze again. Describe the actual change in scores without promising that DE improves this particular example.

**Final 20 seconds:** Open Evolution Lab, select Rastrigin, run, pause with **Space**, and inspect the timeline. Explain the population points and objective. Distinguish this mathematical search from replaying the separate CNN experiment. Return to the slides.

If a checkpoint is unavailable or analysis fails, acknowledge the state. Use `demo/demo.gif` only as an identified recording, never as a substitute claimed to be live inference.

### 17. Explain the metrics — Sushant

“Accuracy measures correct labels; macro-F1 weights classes equally, and ROC-AUC measures ranking. Calibration is different: DE’s seed-zero ECE is 9.23%, worse than baseline 0.81%, mainly from underconfidence. A high-accuracy model can still give poorly calibrated probabilities. No single metric establishes field readiness.”

### 18. Explain uncertainty and paired evidence — Sushant

“Bootstrap intervals use one thousand resamples of seed-zero predictions. McNemar finds 60 images correct only under DE and 3 correct only under the baseline, with p about 9.05e-15. This supports their clean-test difference. It does not compare DE with random search, or measure uncertainty across new farms.”

Handoff: “Richik will separate optimizer validation from classifier evaluation and explain the computational cost.”

### 19. Compare benchmark convergence — Richik

“The completed analytic study uses Sphere, Rosenbrock, Rastrigin, Ackley, and Griewank. DE, jDE, GA, PSO, and random search receive equal function-evaluation budgets across all registered seeds. Curves show median best-so-far error with interquartile spread. This tests the optimizer independently of the expensive CNN objective.”

Point to the budget and dimension printed on the slide. The two-dimensional landscapes in the demo are explanatory; the main quantitative study is ten-dimensional.

### 20. Read the median table — Richik

“Lower final error is better. DE has the lowest median on Rosenbrock; PSO on Sphere and Ackley; jDE on Rastrigin and Griewank. This is landscape-dependent evidence, not a universal ranking. The report also shows mean, standard deviation, best runs, and exploratory rank-sum outputs.”

If asked about those tests: “They are unadjusted for multiple comparisons and not tie-corrected, despite some ties. We do not treat their approximate p-values as confirmatory significance evidence.”

### 21. Explain measured computation — Richik

“DE takes more final-training time than either comparator, while their warmed forward latencies are similar. This slide sums final training across all seeds; the report also shows per-seed mean and spread. Search duration sums candidate-training time. The CPU demo adds decoding, Grad-CAM, and interface work, so its response time is different.”

### 22. State advantages and limitations — Aryanraj

“Strengths include auditable operators, fixed protocols, equal budgets, resumable execution, and offline inference. Limitations include controlled imagery, one crop and split, and one CNN search per method. Final seeds measure variation for selected recipes, not independent searches. The clean-accuracy tie and worse DE robustness and calibration are explicit results.”

Validate unknown-input handling and on-device export. These require new protected evaluation data; we must not retune against the test results just reported.”
